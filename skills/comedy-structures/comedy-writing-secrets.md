## Part I: The Basics of Humor Writing

### Chapter 1: The Importance of Humor Writing

**Core thesis:** Humor is not a mystery—it has structure and formula. You can learn this creative art.

> "What is comedy? Comedy is the art of making people laugh without making them puke." —Steve Martin

#### The Three Rs of Humor

The benefits of humor writing are:

1. **Respect:** Humor earns you favorable attention. "Laughter makes your psyche healthy and bright and vigorous." Unlike other art forms, humor offers immediate gratification—you know within a half-second if the audience appreciates it.

2. **Remembrance:** When we're successfully humorous, people remember. Our best lines are retained and repeated. Studies show students who attend lectures with humor achieve higher test scores.

3. **Rewards:** Humor writing is commanding more attention and financial opportunity than ever before.

#### The MAP Theory

**MAP** = Material, Audience, Performer

Successful humor requires all three elements working together:

- **Material:** The content of the humor itself
- **Audience:** Who you're writing for—their hostilities, interests, and expectations
- **Performer:** The persona delivering the material (or the writer's voice)

The MAP is a triangle—of the three, the audience is the most important consideration. Material and performer must adapt to the audience, not the other way around.

### Chapter 2: Why We Laugh

Four major theories explain why we laugh:

#### 1. Superiority Theory
We laugh at the misfortunes of others because we feel superior. As Mel Brooks said, "Tragedy is if I cut my finger. Comedy is if you drop into an open sewer and die."

#### 2. Incongruity Theory
Humor arises from the unexpected—a disconnect between what we anticipate and what occurs. The brain enjoys resolving incongruities.

#### 3. Surprise Theory
The sudden perception of incongruity creates surprise, which triggers laughter. The element of the unexpected is crucial.

#### 4. Release Theory
Laughter releases tension. Humor serves as a socially acceptable way to express forbidden thoughts and feelings.

**Key insight:** Most humor involves some combination of all four theories.

### Chapter 3: The Recipe for Humor (THREES)

There are six essential ingredients in any recipe for humor. The acronym **THREES** makes them memorable:

- **T**arget
- **H**ostility
- **R**ealism
- **E**xaggeration
- **E**motion
- **S**urprise

#### Target: Aiming Your Humor

> "If there's no corpse, there's usually no joke." —Mike Sankey

Humor is criticism cloaked as entertainment and directed at a specific target. Target selection is arguably the most critical factor in writing commercially successful humor.

**Universal targets work best.** Your companion may be the most bizarre person in history, but no one else cares about your partner other than your family members. Unless the audience can vicariously share your experiences, your material won't land.

**Common targets:**

1. **Yourself** — The least offensive but most effective target. "Inviting people to laugh with you while you are laughing at yourself is a good thing. You may be the fool, but you're the fool in charge." —Carl Reiner

2. **Sex** — Close to 25% of all humor. We're all ambivalent about sexual activity—frustrated by exaggerated reports of adequacy.

3. **Celebrities** — Our appetite for gossip about heroes, icons, and villains is insatiable. No sooner do the famous reach the apex than we humble them with humor.

4. **Places** — We ridicule countries, states, cities to feel superior. Every humorist has a favorite dumping ground.

5. **Products** — The basic rule: Your target must be an object of annoyance shared by the entire audience.

6. **Ideas** — Religion, politics, meaning of life. Most likely to backfire because a person's politics aren't visible like clothes.

#### Hostility: Ridicruel

> "Comedy is cruel. The words cruel and ridicule appear together frequently—where there is one, there is also the other."

Humor is a powerful antidote to hostile feelings. We use humor to express criticism we couldn't otherwise voice.

**Sources of hostility (and therefore humor):**

1. **Authority** — We enjoy pricking the bloated arrogance of authority. Humor is almost always directed upward—attacking those perceived as superior. "I looked up the word politics in the dictionary, and it's actually a combination of two words: poli, which means 'many,' and tics, which means 'bloodsuckers.'" —Jay Leno

2. **Sex** — Both genders are insecure about performance and satisfaction.

3. **Money and Business** — "Someday I want to be rich. Some people get so rich they lose all respect for humanity. That's how rich I want to be." —Rita Rudner

4. **Family** — Hostility against family responsibilities and restrictions needs little explanation.

5. **Angst** — Fears of death, deformity, neurotic symptoms. Woody Allen: "I merchandise misery."

6. **Technology** — Our love-hate relationship with devices that make life simultaneously easier and more frustrating.

7. **Group Differences** — Gender, age, ethnicity, politics—though increasingly treacherous territory.

#### Realism: Raise Your Sites

> "Most good jokes state a bitter truth." —Larry Gelbart

Without some fundamental basis of truth, there's little with which the audience can associate. But jokes also bend the truth, and the challenge is to learn how to tell the truth (be realistic) while lying (exaggerating).

The basic two-step in humor is to:
1. State some common problem, frequently with a cliché
2. Create an unexpected ending or surprise

> "If you've never wanted to kill your mate, you've never been in love. If you've never held a box of rat poison in your hand and stared at it for a good long while, you've never been in love." —Chris Rock

**Dorothy Parker:** "The difference between wit and wisecracking is that wit has truth to it, while wisecracking is simply calisthenics with words."

#### Exaggeration: Talking Up a Storm

Only for humor is the public willing to suspend disbelief. We permit humorists to utilize hyperbole, blatant distortion, and overstated figures that signal: *Hey, it's only a joke.*

**The likely next to the unlikely:** A newspaper ran photos of a matron elected president of the Women's Republican Club and a gorilla new to the local zoo—but the captions got switched. That's likely. The newspaper got sued for defamation—by the gorilla! That's unlikely.

#### Emotion: Burst the Bubble

There must be a buildup of anticipation. Think of hostility as an inflated balloon. When you create tension in your audience, you're adding more air, building anticipation over when the balloon will burst.

**Techniques for building emotion:**

1. **The pregnant pause** — Pausing just before the payoff word. Even in Henny Youngman's classic, "Take my wife—please!" the slight pause is essential.

2. **Ask a question** — "Anybody see this commercial on TV last night?" encourages involvement.

3. **The build** — A joke that leads to a joke that leads to another joke. Together they prepare for one big blast.

4. **Work the audience** — Walking into the audience builds fear in each member that they may be the next victim.

#### Surprise: Nobody Knows the Stumbles I've Seen

> "Comedy is mentally pulling the rug out from under each person in your audience. But first, you have to get them to stand on it. You have to fool them, because if they see you preparing to tug on the rug, they'll move." —Gene Perret

Charlie Chaplin's definition: The villain is chasing the heroine. On the sidewalk is a banana peel. Camera cuts back and forth. At the last second, the heavy sees the banana peel, jumps over it—and falls into an open manhole.

**If the audience can see the surprise coming, they're not surprised, just smug.**

## Part II: Humor Writing Techniques

### Chapter 4: POW - Play on Words

More than 50% of all humor is based on plays on words (POWs).

> "My wife made me join a bridge club. I jump off next week." —Rodney Dangerfield

A POW is a twist on a familiar cliché, aphorism, title, famous quote, or any expression widely known by the public. It can use double entendres, homonyms, or puns.

#### Types of POW

**1. Double Entendres**
Words or phrases that can be interpreted two ways—one of which is usually risqué.

> "The quickest way to a man's heart is through his chest." —Roseanne Barr

**2. Homonyms**
Words that sound alike but have different meanings.

> "At the gym they have free weights, so I took them." —Steve Smith

> "If you shoot a mime, should you use a silencer?" —Steven Wright

**3. Malapropisms**
Using the wrong word that sounds similar to the right one.

**4. Oxymorons**
Combining contradictory terms: military intelligence, jumbo shrimp, act naturally, government organization.

**5. Puns**
The lowest form of humor... and the foundation of the highest. A pun substitutes a word with another word that sounds the same but has a different meaning.

**6. Cliché Twists**
Taking a familiar saying and altering it unexpectedly.

> "If at first you don't succeed, try, try again. Then give up. There's no sense being a damn fool about it." —W.C. Fields

**7. Reforming**
Altering a well-known phrase slightly.

Original: "Rite of Spring"
Reformed: "Write of Spring," "Right of Spring," "Wright of Spring"

#### The Simple Truth

Stating the obvious that nobody says out loud.

> "Sincerity—if you can fake that, you've got it made." —George Burns

The humor comes from acknowledging what everyone knows but is too polite to say.

#### The Take-Off

Starting with something familiar and veering unexpectedly.

> "Two roads diverged in a wood and I took the road less traveled by... state troopers."

### Chapter 5: More POW - The Simple Truth and Take-Off

#### Observational Humor

The "Did you ever notice..." formula. Pointing out the absurdities in everyday life.

> "If the world is normal, then how come hot dogs come in packages of ten and hot dog buns come in packages of eight?" —Robert Wohl

#### What If? Technique

Imagination allows you to realign diverse elements into new and unexpected relationships that surprise.

- What if mother's milk was declared a health hazard? Where would they put the warning label?
- What if you actually saw McNuggets on a chicken?
- What if alphabet soup consistently spelled out obscene words?

**The cardinal rule: Don't be inhibited.** It's better to take a nihilistic attitude toward sensitive subjects than to pussyfoot around taboos. Editing and self-censorship are second and third steps—never the first.

### Chapter 6: POW Brainstorming Techniques

> "Writer's block is a fancy term made up by whiners so they can have an excuse to drink alcohol." —Steve Martin

#### The Rule of Ten

For every ten jokes written, only one might be acceptable. This explains why late-night shows employ teams of gag writers—a five-minute monologue may be written by six writers.

#### Association

Putting two activities that haven't been previously associated into a plausible but audacious scenario.

> "My opponent has done the work of two men: Laurel and Hardy." —Governor James A. Rhodes

**Types of association:**

1. **Cliché + unexpected example** — Begin with a cliché the audience interprets one way, then give an example that reverses the meaning.

2. **Teaming two clichés** — The backbone of improvisation.

3. **Tom Swifty** — A quotation paired with a verb/adverb that puns on the meaning.
   - "I want to renew my membership," Tom rejoined.
   - "I hope I can still play the guitar," he fretted.
   - "All the twos are missing from this deck," she deduced.

#### The Warm-Up Exercise

Robert Orben writes 25 POW jokes inspired by the morning paper before getting to work. Others imagine funny captions to news photos.

#### Listing

Break down a topic into groups of related activities, then create smaller subgroups. For golf:
1. Golf equipment (clubs, balls, bag, cart, clothes...)
2. Golf course (fairway, green, sand trap, nineteenth hole...)
3. Golf play (birdie, bogey, hole in one, mulligan...)
4. Golf players (hackers, hustlers, sandbaggers...)

Then list clichés for each: "got ya by the balls," "that's not his bag," "let's play a round..."

#### Revising for Surprise

Jokes aren't written—they're rewritten.

**A well-constructed joke:**
- Uses as few words as possible
- Does not reveal key words in the setup
- Preserves the funniest word until the end

**Cutting example:**

First draft: "On the road into town there was a sign in an empty field that said, 'Three miles ahead, lots for sale.' So I went to the location, but to my surprise, there was nothing there."

Final draft: "I saw this sign: 'Lots for sale.' But there was nothing there."

**Make every word work.**

### Chapter 7: Reverses

> "My boyfriend and I broke up, even though we're still deeply in love. He wanted to get married and I didn't want him to." —Rita Rudner

A reverse is "a device that adds a contradictory tag line to the opening line of a standard expression or cliché."

> "I couldn't wait for success, so I went ahead without it." —Jonathan Winters

The most common definition: "an unexpected switch in the audience's point of view." Surprise comes from a basic change in direction—a reversal of habitual thinking.

#### Misdirection

To maintain surprise, you must drop at least one prominent clue to mislead the audience:

> A man and woman are making passionate love in the bedroom. Suddenly, the apartment door opens, and a man comes in and shouts, "Darling! I'm home." He walks into the bedroom, sees the naked couple and says, "What is she doing here?"

The misdirection is the man shouting "Darling!"—the audience thinks he's calling to the woman. The unexpected change occurs in the last line.

#### More Reverse Examples

> "I sold my house this week. I got a good price for it—but it made my landlord mad as hell." —Garry Shandling

> "When I was young, I thought that money was the most important thing in life. Now that I'm old—I know it is." —Oscar Wilde

> "I made a killing in the stock market. My broker lost all my money, so I killed him." —Jim Loy

#### The Emo Philips Masterclass

> "One day I was playing—I was about seven years old—and I saw the cellar door open just a crack. Now my folks had always warned me: Emo, whatever you do, don't go near the cellar door. But I had to see what was on the other side if it killed me, so I went to the cellar door, pushed it open and walked through, and I saw strange, wonderful things—things I had never seen before—like... trees, grass, flowers, the sun—that was nice!"

The audience assumes he's outside the cellar looking in. The clues build tension ("warned me," "if it killed me," "strange things"). The reveal: He was inside the cellar looking out.

#### The Anecdotal Reverse

A short story with a sudden climax. The setup includes just enough information to encourage the audience to proceed automatically in a direction you reverse at the end.

> A man was driving on a narrow, winding mountain road when he almost collided with a car that wildly careened around a blind curve. "You stupid fool," he shouted at the other driver. The other car came to a dead stop. A woman rolled down the window, looked at the man and yelled, "Pig! Pig! Pig!" and then quickly drove off. Furious at the insult, the man slammed his car into gear, roared around the mountain curve—and slammed head-on into a giant hog standing in the road.

#### Don't Telegraph

Telegraphing—inadvertently cluing the audience in—is a sign of a beginner. Too-detailed introductions make the setup obvious so the audience anticipates the ending.

**There is an unwritten law that only one reverse is permissible in any one anecdote.** Two is pushing it, and the audience can usually predict a third.

#### Hiding

The opposite of telegraphing. The audience believes the setup to be a straightforward statement. After a short pause, the humorist reveals the surprise ending.

> "Ohio University was founded in 1804 and opened with a freshman class of twelve students. And this year, eight of them graduated."

### Chapter 8: Paired Elements

Paired elements appear in humor formats as varied as ad slogans, standup, cartoons, and greeting cards. They rely on juxtaposition—placing contrasting ideas side by side.

> "Men should be like Kleenex: soft, strong and disposable." —Cher

> "Making love to a woman is like buying real estate: location, location, location." —Carol Leifer

#### Paired Phrases

Clichés afford many opportunities:

- "To be or not to be" → "To pee or not to pee"
- "To err is human, to forgive divine" → "To err is human, to blame it on someone else shows management potential"

#### Paired Antonyms

Contrasting opposites: hot vs. cold, tall vs. short, rich vs. poor.

> "I like Florida; everything is in the eighties: the temperature, the ages, and the IQs." —George Carlin

#### Writing Backward

Paired elements are often written backward. Start with the punchline, then craft the setup that makes it work.

### Chapter 9: Triples

> "I can't think of anything worse after a night of drinking than waking up next to someone and not being able to remember their name, or how you met, or why they're dead." —Laura Kightlinger

Every joke structure has its devotees, but the triple is one format all humorists use repeatedly. Featuring a grouping of three examples or a sequence of three actions, the triple increases tension with its longer buildup.

> "I celebrated Thanksgiving in an old-fashioned way. I invited everyone in my neighborhood to my house, we had an enormous feast, and then I killed them and took their land." —Jon Stewart

#### The Power of Threes

The mystical power of three has been known for centuries:
- Three wise men, the Trinity
- "Life, liberty, and the pursuit of happiness"
- "Of the people, for the people, by the people"
- Goldilocks and the three bears
- Three blind mice
- Three little pigs
- Three Stooges

Three may be an odd number in math, but its even da-da-TA da-da-TA da-da-TA cadence makes it the most important number in comedy.

#### The SAP Test

According to William Lang's theory, there are only three parts to most comedic bits:

- **S** = Setup (preparation)
- **A** = Anticipation (triple)
- **P** = Punchline (story payoff)

Example:
- S = "We were Pentecostal."
- A = "When I was growing up we couldn't go to movies, we couldn't listen to rock music, we couldn't wear makeup."
- P = "That's just a lightbulb and a car away from being Amish." —René Hicks

#### Triple Examples

> "When you die there's a light at the end of the tunnel. When my father dies, he'll see the light, make his way toward it, and then flip it off to save electricity." —Harland Williams

> "If you want to be seen—stand up!
> If you want to be heard—speak up!
> If you want to be appreciated—shut up!"

The joke wouldn't be as effective as a series of two. The third element provides the punch.

#### The Lightbulb Format

How many ___s does it take to change a lightbulb?

The triple format:

> How many Louisianans does it take?
> Three: one to hold the ladder, one to screw in the bulb, and one to bribe officials for the permit.

> How many Oregonians?
> Forty-two: one to hold the ladder, one to screw in the bulb, and forty to draft the environmental impact statement.

#### Three Rules About Three

1. Never use more than three jokes about one subject in a monologue.
2. Three minutes is the ideal length for a skit.
3. Don't exceed three themes in an article.

### Chapter 10: Realism, Exaggeration, and Understatement

> "When a thing is funny, search it for a hidden truth." —George Bernard Shaw

Nowhere is structure more evident than in the interaction of realism and exaggeration. They balance each other like equal weights on a scale.

> "My dad's pants kept creeping up on him. By sixty-five he was just a pair of pants and a head." —Jeff Altman

In this joke, the realistic element is the rising waistband. The exaggeration is the waistband reaching the head.

#### Make It Real

> "My one rule is to be true, rather than funny." —Bill Cosby

The more realistic we make the humor seem, the more our audience identifies with it. Truth involves the audience—this is why people want to know the latest news.

A premise must be true and interesting to the audience, not just you, the writer.

> "He's the kind of bore who, when you meet him on the street and say, 'How are you?' he stops and tells you."

**50% of the time people don't care about your problems, and the other 50% they're glad you're getting what you deserve.**

#### The Stretch-Band Theory

Exaggeration is the Silly Putty of humor writing. You start with a realistic scenario, then bend and distort it for comic effect.

Think of humor as a rubber band:
1. Unstretched band = realism
2. Stretching = exaggeration
3. Tension increases as you stretch
4. The sound when plucked = emotion in the audience

**Key rule:** The audience must understand the connection between the truth and the exaggeration. There can be too much distortion.

> "If I've told you once, I've told you a thousand times: Don't exaggerate."

#### Overstatement vs. Understatement

**Overstatement (Hyperbole):**
> "The scarecrow scared the crows so badly that they brought back the corn they had stolen two years before." —Fred Allen

**Understatement:**
> "I have my standards. They may be low, but I have them." —Bette Midler

> "I have low self-esteem. When I'm in bed with someone, I fantasize that I'm someone else." —Richard Lewis

#### The TEE Test

Three criteria determine whether a premise properly sets up the punchline:

- **T** = Truth: Reality-based, genuine, true
- **E** = Emotion: Stated or implied hostility about the stupidity, absurdity, or weirdness of the premise
- **E** = Explicitness: Specific and readily understood by others

#### Good, Better, Best

The humor writer tries to determine how far in any direction the truth can be exaggerated without destroying credibility.

A prisoner calling his companion:
- a "cellmate" is **good**
- a "roommate" is **better**
- a "suite-mate" is **best**

Realism is frequently funniest when exaggerated to the most extreme possibility. In math, 1 + 1 = 2. In humor, 1 + 1 = 11.

### Chapter 11: Funny Words and Foul Language

Not all words are created equal. Some words are inherently funnier than others.

#### The K Sound

Words with hard K sounds are funnier: pickle, chicken, kumquat, Cucamonga.

> "Keokuk, Kokomo, Kankakee—those are funny names. You put a K in front of something and it becomes funny." —Neil Simon

#### Hard Consonants

Generally funnier than soft ones: P, B, T, D, K, G create harder sounds.

#### Word Length

Short, punchy words often hit harder than long ones. But unexpected long words in casual contexts can also be funny.

#### Timing Words

Words that force you to pause or stress differently create rhythm in jokes.

#### Foul Language

Profanity can add punch but becomes a crutch if overused. The shock value diminishes with repetition. Use sparingly for maximum effect.

## Summary: The Complete Checklist

### Before Writing

1. Identify your target (universal, not personal)
2. Confirm your audience shares hostility toward the target
3. Check: Is this true enough to be relatable?

### While Writing

4. Exaggerate logically (stretch, don't snap)
5. Build emotional tension with pacing
6. Hide the surprise until the last possible word
7. Use one of the 13 structures

### While Editing

8. Cut every nonessential phrase
9. Cut every nonessential word
10. Move the funny word to the end
11. Don't telegraph
12. Test: Would someone else find this funny?

### The 13 Structures

1. **Reverse** — Contradict at the end
2. **Triple** — Two normal + one weird
3. **POW** — Twist the familiar phrase
4. **Paired Elements** — Unexpected parallel
5. **Exaggeration** — Stretch to breaking point
6. **Understatement** — Minimize the big
7. **Misdirect** — False assumption → reveal
8. **Simple Truth** — Say the unsaid
9. **Callback** — Reference earlier joke
10. **Build** — Small → medium → big
11. **Take-Off** — Familiar → tangent
12. **Comparison** — A is like B (surprising B)
13. **Self-Deprecation** — You're the target

## Final Word

> "You can teach literate people anything, from Einstein's theory of relativity to how to play shortstop. And compared to humor writing secrets, playing the piano's eighty-eight keys or speaking Greek is a lot harder to learn and a lot less fun."

Comedy is more art than science, but it's an art that can be learned through structure and practice. The rule of ten applies: For every ten jokes written, only one might be acceptable. But with practice, your ratio improves.

**The mantra:** Nothing stinks. Nothing does stink! Don't let your internal critic limit your imagination. Write freely first. Edit ruthlessly second.

> "Humor writing demands practice. If you're not funny by then, demand your money back and don't ever get married."
